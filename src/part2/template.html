⚔️ Competitors keep raising the bar

{{COMP_CHART}}
We ask an LLM agent to run a startup in a simulator for 500 days. It's a typical subscription-based AI software company, and running it means coordinating many moving parts.
Pricing: it has to decide the price for three subscription tiers.
Growth: it allocates ad budget into different channels for each customer group.
And each customer group responds differently to each channel, which is hidden from the agent.
Product quality: different customer groups have different quality bars, and they are willing to pay more as quality increases.
The agent can improve quality with R&D, which costs money and has delayed impact.
Other factors like quota limits for each plan and service reliability also impact quality.
Enterprise sales: it negotiates with enterprise customers over multiple turns, whose true price preference and maximum rounds of negotiation aren't revealed.
Competitors: they increase customers' quality expectations over time, so there's always pressure to improve product quality.

How do we make CEO-Bench rigorous and challenging? First, we maximize realism with granular simulation.
There are different customer groups…
…and each customer is simulated individually…
…with its own distinct preferences, sampled from its group.
All of this is hidden from the agent.
What it gets instead is business databases, and it has to write data analysis code to infer what's going on.
How we make CEO-Bench rigorous and challenging
Robust simulation through mechanistic rules ⚙️
{{PQ_SVG}}
When the simulation isn't robust, agents spiral 🌀
Second, the simulation is robust because it runs on mechanistic rules.
For example, a price-versus-quality curve keeps customer behavior consistent: offer enough quality for the price and they subscribe, otherwise they reject.
Every customer follows the same rule, so there's no way to talk your way past it. Compare that with Vending-Bench, where an agent ended up emailing the FBI.
